DETROIT AUTOMATION ACADEMY · A LIBRARY

Arcadia

Old Macs, running the whole history of games, film, and sound — lawfully. Arcadia is the Academy’s library for machines on macOS High Sierra (10.13): the emulators that still run on them, and shelves of public-domain film, music, books, and homebrew games, with every title’s rights checked before it is listed.

THE LIBRARY

Two shelves, one standard: if it isn’t lawful, it isn’t here.

A Mac from 2012 is not a museum piece — it is a machine that can still play fifty years of games and a century of film. Arcadia keeps the list of what runs, and the shelf of what may be shared, in one place.

THE MACHINES

Emulators for High Sierra

Emulators are lawful software, and Arcadia lists them freely: which ones still run on macOS 10.13, and exactly which version is the last one that does. A version is named here only when that has been checked against the maker’s own records.

THE SHELVES

Film, sound, books & homebrew games

The shelves carry works anyone may share: public-domain film and recordings, books whose copyright has run its course, and games their makers released to be shared. Each title is admitted with its rights written down beside it — never on a stranger’s say-so.

VERIFIED SO FAR

Checked, not guessed.

These two entries are confirmed against their makers’ own records. The rest of the list is still being checked, version by version — and nothing joins this page as settled fact until the check is done.

FILM & MEDIA

How a work earns its place on the shelf.

“Public domain” is not one thing. Arcadia keeps the classes separate, and a work is shelved under the class its own paperwork proves.

Free by age

Works published in 1930 or earlier are public domain in the United States. The early shelf already reads like a history of cinema: Metropolis, Nosferatu, The General, The Kid, Safety Last!, Sherlock Jr., Battleship Potemkin, and Steamboat Willie.

Free by lapse

Some later works entered the public domain because a copyright was never renewed, or was filed with a defective notice. These are admitted one title at a time, and only when the evidence for that title is on file — a famous name on a list somewhere is not evidence.

Works of the government

Works of the United States government are public domain by law — the films, photographs, and recordings the public already paid for. Open collections released under Creative Commons licenses are listed under those licenses, exactly as their makers set them; a CC0 release is called CC0, never relabeled.

The library reads from the great open shelves: Project Gutenberg, Smithsonian Open Access, the Library of Congress’s “Free to Use and Reuse” sets, The Public Domain Review, NASA’s image library, and the Prelinger Archives — each Prelinger film checked on its own, because their rights vary film by film.

Two standing cautions, applied to every title: television is judged episode by episode, never a whole series on one claim; and music carries two rights at once — the song and the recording of it — so both must be clear before a recording is shelved.

THE RULES OF THE HOUSE

The legal fence is the identity of this library.

Arcadia exists to prove a library like this can be built without taking anything. These rules do not bend:

  • Emulators are listed freely — they are lawful software.
  • The shelves carry homebrew, public-domain, and openly-licensed works only, each with its license traced to its maker’s own release.
  • Copyrighted commercial games are never hosted, distributed, or linked here. If you own the game, you bring your own copy, made from your own disc or cartridge — the library can explain how; it will not hand you the file.
  • System files an emulator may need — BIOS, firmware, and classic Mac ROM images — are likewise yours to supply. They are copyrighted, and they never pass through this library.
  • Free to download does not mean free to redistribute. A title is shelved only when its grant permits listing it here, and the grant is recorded beside the title.
