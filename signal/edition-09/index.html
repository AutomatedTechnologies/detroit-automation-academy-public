The Route Home

Published Wednesday, November 11, 2026 · By Justin Spencer Smith · Also on LinkedIn

Wednesday, November 11, is Veterans Day. By the Census Bureau's count, 64,943 veterans live in Wayne County alone — more people than fill Comerica Park and Ford Field's lower bowl combined, most of them riding the same buses and trains as everyone else, to the same appointments, interviews, and classrooms. The Signal covers the ride. So this edition is about the ride, measured the way this newsletter measures everything: by what actually ran, what it cost, and who it carried.

The parade that didn't march

Start with the story nobody planned. Detroit's Veterans Day Parade — the twenty-first annual — did not step off this year. Its route down Michigan Avenue through Corktown is a construction zone: the state is spending some $70 million rebuilding two miles of US-12 between I-96 and Woodward, brick by brick, through 2027. So this year's tribute moves indoors, into the IBEW Local 58 hall on Abbott Street — scheduled for Sunday, November 8, doors at 9:30, ceremonies at 11:00, with registration closed at capacity before the doors ever opened, by the organizers' own posting — and the 4Star 4Mile runners run their Corktown loop without the procession they usually run beside. There is a whole philosophy in that detour. A parade is a route a city closes so honor can pass through it. This year the route itself is being rebuilt, so that it can carry the next fifty years of them. Detroit honors its veterans in a union hall on a side street, two blocks from the torn-up avenue, and the avenue is torn up because the city finally decided it was worth rebuilding. Both halves of that sentence are the tribute.

Eight days after the fare went to zero

On Tuesday, November 3 — Election Day — DDOT and SMART waived every fare, all day, so anyone who needed to reach a polling place could. No pass, no proof, no transaction at the farebox. It was the region saying, in the plainest language a transit system has, that the trip was part of the civic act. Hold that fact next to this one: the people honored on Wednesday are the people who stood guard over the right exercised on Tuesday. The same buses, the same fareboxes, the same operators, eight days apart. Detroit already owns the machinery for making a ride free when the day means something. It ran that machinery eight days ago.

The ride to care

The least visible transit system in this region belongs to the VA. The John D. Dingell VA Medical Center — 4646 John R, at Canfield in Midtown, named for a Detroit congressman who wore the Army uniform in the Second World War before he ever wore the title — sits one block east of Woodward Avenue, the region's most-served transit spine, where the QLINE runs at no fare and Live Transit Detroit tracks every vehicle live. Around that fixed route, volunteers run a shadow network: DAV vans, driven by volunteers, carrying veterans to appointments at no cost; the VA's own Veterans Transportation Service for riders whose medical condition requires a special vehicle, dispatched at (313) 576-4346; Macomb County Veterans Services shuttling veterans from their homes to morning appointments at the medical center, booked at (586) 469-5315. None of it appears on a route map. All of it is transit — scheduled, dispatched, load-bearing. Care that people cannot reach is care they do not get, and in this region a quiet fleet of vans and volunteers exists precisely so the reach is not the reason.

What is already on the books

Oakland County has the standing version. Since 2024, the county and SMART have run This Ride's On Us: free paratransit and connector rides for veterans who live in the county — and for their dependents and their widows and widowers. Medical appointments, job interviews, educational classes, community events: the trips that hold a life together. Registration runs through Oakland County Veterans Services at (248) 419-7984, and rides are booked ahead. It is not a ceremony. It is a weekday-morning ride that costs nothing, booked like any other trip. That is what a benefit looks like when it is built as transit instead of applause.

The honest gap

Said plainly, because this newsletter says things plainly: the Dart pass — the regional pass covering DDOT, SMART, and the QLINE — sets its reduced fares by age, disability, Medicare, and student status. Veteran status alone is not on the list. That is not an accusation; it is the map as drawn, effective January 26, 2026, and a map is the first thing you need before you argue about redrawing it. A region that waived every fare for one civic Tuesday can hold this conversation with a straight face. This edition is the Academy holding its share of it, on the day it matters most, with the receipts below.

The close

Gratitude is cheap as a word and expensive as a system. Detroit's version, at its best, is concrete: a fare waived on the day it counts, a van dispatched to the appointment, a tribute moved indoors when the parade route couldn't be marched, an avenue rebuilt so the procession has somewhere to march next year. The Signal publishes on Fridays; this week it publishes on Wednesday, on purpose. To every reader who served — all 64,943 of you in this county, and the families who served alongside you in the way families do — thank you. The route home is yours. We will keep it running, and we will keep it counted.
