The Mountain Lion Machine

Published Saturday, November 8, 2026 · By Justin Spencer Smith · Also on LinkedIn

A support group for unsupported machines.

Our main development machine is a MacBook Air that shipped in June 2012 and started its working life on OS X Mountain Lion. It now runs an operating system Apple last patched on November 12, 2020 — six years ago next week. This month that machine climbed three operating systems in place, took a current browser, launched a network scanner we rebuilt for it, and found seventeen live networks in its first scan. Nobody at Apple supports any part of that sentence. We do. This series is the record of how.

The machine

The MacBook Air on the bench is a 13-inch Mid-2012 — a 1.7 GHz Intel Core i5 with four gigabytes of memory, which is to say the ordinary machine of its year, not a preserved flagship. It began on OS X 10.8, Mountain Lion, and for years that is where it stayed, the way most old machines stay: not broken, just behind. On November 6 it completed an in-place climb — 10.8 to El Capitan to High Sierra, macOS 10.13.6 — its own files, its own history, no wipe. Fourteen years after it was built, it is the verification rig for our legacy software program: the machine every build has to answer to.

The obituary

Apple writes an obituary for every product, and it is always written in dates. High Sierra shipped September 25, 2017. Its final security update landed November 12, 2020. Even Catalina — the newest system Apple permits this machine to install — received its last security update in July 2022. The browser the estate treats as its floor, Safari 11.1, dates to March 2018. The current macOS is Tahoe 26, eight generations downstream of the system this laptop runs. Read plainly: the manufacturer has not stood behind this laptop's software for six years, nor behind the newest software it is allowed to run for four. The hardware never got the memo. It boots and holds a charge. What expired was not the machine. What expired was the support.

The work

Closing that gap is labor, and labor is the part an AI agent changes. The process that put this Air back in service was not a trick or a patcher. It was maintenance, done in order: a full security audit before any publication, findings written down where anyone can read them; a browser chosen for the floor — Chromium Legacy 127, a current engine that still speaks to a 2017 system; a Muse desktop client rebuilt on an older Electron frame because the official application requires a Mac from 2024; and the software itself — KisMac, abandoned upstream since 2018 — audited line by line, its findings remediated, its builds compiled for the old system and launched on this very laptop. First launch: a live scan, seventeen real networks, current timestamps. The agent supplies the hours. The human supplies the judgment, and verifies with his own eyes.

The arithmetic

Total spent on the resurrection: zero dollars. The whole legacy program runs under a budget freeze, and the freeze turned out to be the method — when you cannot buy your way forward, you maintain your way forward. The counter-math is the industry's business model: a replacement laptop is a four-figure purchase and a working machine in a landfill. This one costs nothing per month and its cost per year of service falls every month it stays on the desk. Multiply by the router still carrying our house (Edition 2), the four abandoned applications back under maintenance (Edition 3), and a website engineered for a 2018 browser (Edition 4), and the series has its thesis: the upgrade cycle is a policy, and policy can be declined.

The close

Support is a decision, not a date. A manufacturer can publish an end of life; it cannot make a cared-for machine stop being useful — only neglected. So the machines have formed a group, and this newsletter is where it meets. Fourteen years old, six years unsupported, in service this morning. That is not nostalgia. That is infrastructure, kept.
